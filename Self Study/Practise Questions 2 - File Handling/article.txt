The Return of the River Otter

For decades, the river otter was absent from the streams of eastern Ontario. Trapping, pollution, and the loss of wetland habitat pushed the animal out of much of its former range. Today, the otter is back, and biologists say the recovery is one of the quiet conservation stories of the region.

"We started seeing tracks along the riverbank about fifteen years ago," said a field researcher who has studied the population since the early 2000s. "At first, we thought it was a mink. Then the camera traps caught a family of four sliding down a muddy slope, and we knew the otters had returned."

The recovery did not happen by accident. Cleaner water was the first step. Stricter rules on industrial waste, along with upgrades to municipal treatment plants, reduced the toxins that once built up in fish. Since otters eat mostly fish, cleaner fish meant healthier otters.

Wetland restoration was the second step. Volunteers planted native grasses, removed invasive reeds, and rebuilt beaver ponds that had been drained for farmland. Otters often use old beaver lodges as dens, so the return of the beaver helped the otter, too.

Not everyone welcomes the change. Some anglers worry that otters will empty their favourite fishing holes. Researchers say the evidence does not support that fear. An otter eats roughly a kilogram of fish a day, and most of its diet is made up of slow, small species, not the trout and bass that anglers prize.

Otters are also a sign of a healthy river. Because they sit near the top of the food chain, they reflect the condition of everything below them: the insects, the crayfish, the fish, and the water itself. When otters thrive, the whole river is usually doing well.

The next challenge is roads. As the population grows, young otters travel farther to find new territory, and many are struck by cars where rivers pass under highways. Some towns have started building wildlife underpasses, small tunnels beside the water that let animals cross safely.

For now, the otter's future looks bright. Residents report sightings at dawn and dusk, and children on school trips press their faces to the glass at the nature centre, hoping to see a sleek brown head break the surface of the pond.

The river otter, once gone, is home again.
